EcoBean

co-founder & CEO, 2025–26

A plant-growing kit made from recycled cans, cardboard, and newspaper, fed with a fertilizer we brewed from used coffee grounds. We built it so elementary and middle schoolers could turn what they learn about climate change into something they can actually grow.

I led a 30-person team across marketing, finance, HR/PR, and production. The fertilizer took rounds of testing to get right, and the box went through a last-week rebuild in the Robotics Lab: laser-cut wood with a 3D-printed water tray. We also ran climate and entrepreneurship workshops for a Girl Scouts troop and an elementary school.

At the Junior Achievement Singapore finals on 24 January 2026, we placed 3rd in the country, judged on our finances, an investor interview, our trade fair booth, and a final report. It was our school's first top-three finish in six years.

Coffee lab

Gold CREST Award, in progress

EcoBean's coffee-ground fertilizer seemed to work, but I wanted to know whether it really did, and why. So I ran a controlled experiment at home and logged every day of it.

It took three runs. Run 1 grew mold in plastic cups; Run 2's seeds never sprouted. Run 3 used proper pots with drainage, fresh red cabbage seeds, and a third pot with store-bought fertilizer to compare against.

Question

Does a liquid coffee-ground fertilizer raise soil nutrients and help microgreens grow?

Pot AWater only (control)
Pot BCoffee fertilizer on Day 5
Pot CStore-bought fertilizer (NPK 1-0-4) on Day 5

Same soil, 2 g of seeds and 30 mL of water a day per pot. Soil tested with LaMotte nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium kits. My parents read the colour tests blind, so I couldn't pick the result I wanted.

Potassium: fewer drops to the endpoint means more potassium.

so… the control won?

The coffee fertilizer did raise nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium above the control. But the control still grew the most. It sat in more sunlight until I rotated the pots on Day 5, and an 8-day run may be too short for extra nutrients to show up as growth. That's exactly what the Gold CREST project is for: longer runs, more pots, and the chemistry behind it.

  • NutrientsUsed grounds carry nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium, plus traces of calcium, magnesium, and iron.
  • Gentle pHBrewing pulls most of the acid into the drink, so used grounds sit near neutral (about 6.5–6.8).
  • Slow releaseSoil microbes break the grounds down and release ammonium; as roots absorb it, equilibrium shifts to release more.
  • Don't overdo itCaffeine at high concentrations can stop seeds germinating, so the dose matters.
